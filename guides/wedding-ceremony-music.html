Ceremony Music Guide

Choosing Your Wedding Ceremony Music

Updated August 2026 · By The StringSisters, Toronto & the GTA

Ceremony music has one job: to carry the room through the moments that matter most, without ever pulling focus from them. This guide walks through how ceremony music is structured, when to choose harp, cello, or both, and where to start with song selection.

How ceremony music is structured

Most ceremonies use the same four musical moments, whether the setting is a downtown hotel ballroom or a backyard in Bond Head. Knowing what each one is for makes song selection much easier — you're not picking songs in the abstract, you're picking the right feeling for a specific 60–90 seconds.

Prelude

Background music as guests arrive and find their seats, typically 15–30 minutes. This is the one stretch where the music can simply set a mood rather than mark a moment — a good place for a few pieces guests will recognize without needing to focus on them.

Processional

The bridal party's entrance, usually building to the couple or one partner walking in last. Many couples use one piece for the wedding party and switch to a second, more significant piece for their own entrance.

Interlude

Optional, and used during a unity ritual, a reading, or signing the register — anywhere the ceremony pauses for something other than speaking. Quiet, unobtrusive instrumental pieces work best here since guests' attention is elsewhere.

Recessional

The newly married couple's exit, and usually the one moment of the ceremony where louder, more celebratory music is not just allowed but expected.

Harp, cello, or both?

The two instruments do genuinely different things, and the right choice usually comes down to the feeling you want in the room rather than a rule about formality.

Harp

Bright, airy, and immediately recognizable as ceremony music — it's the classic choice for a processional because it reads as elegant without effort. It also tends to project cleanly outdoors, since its higher register carries over ambient noise like wind or nearby traffic better than lower-pitched instruments.

Cello

Warmer and more emotionally direct. A cello solo during vows or a signing moment tends to land as more intimate and personal than harp alone — closer to a human voice than any other string instrument.

Both, together

Harp and cello played as a duo cover more sonic range than either alone, which matters most in larger or open-air spaces. It's also simply a fuller sound — most couples who hear both together during a consultation end up choosing the duo over a solo instrument.

Processional song ideas

A mix of classical staples and contemporary pieces arranged for harp and cello — all tested for how they translate acoustically, not just how they sound on the original recording.

  • Canon in D — Pachelbel. The most requested processional piece for a reason: stately, familiar, and works at almost any tempo.
  • Air on the G String — Bach. Slower and more solemn than the Canon — a strong choice for a partner's individual entrance.
  • Clair de Lune — Debussy. Delicate and unhurried, best suited to an indoor or intimate ceremony rather than a large open space.
  • River Flows in You — Yiruma. A contemporary favourite that arranges beautifully for harp; softer and more modern-feeling than the classical staples.
  • A Thousand Years (instrumental) — a common choice for couples who want a modern, cinematic feel without lyrics competing for attention.
  • Here Comes the Sun (instrumental) — a warmer, less formal option for an outdoor or daytime ceremony.

All of these are available in our existing repertoire — no advance arrangement needed.

Recessional song ideas

This is the one place in the ceremony where brighter, faster, more celebratory music is exactly right — the exit is meant to feel like release.

  • Wedding March — Mendelssohn. The traditional recessional, and still a strong, triumphant closer for a classic ceremony.
  • Ode to Joy — Beethoven. Bright and immediately joyful; works especially well arranged for harp and cello together.
  • Can't Help Falling in Love (instrumental) — a gentler, more romantic recessional for couples who want warmth over grandeur.
  • Perfect (instrumental) — Ed Sheeran. A popular modern pick that still feels celebratory without being upbeat in the traditional sense.
  • Signed, Sealed, Delivered I'm Yours (instrumental) — for couples who want the recessional to feel genuinely fun rather than formal.

Timing & practical tips

How long should each piece be?

Aim for pieces that comfortably cover the walk — most processional entrances take 60–90 seconds depending on aisle length and the size of the wedding party. We can loop, extend, or trim any piece live to match exactly how long the walk actually takes, so there's no need to time it perfectly in advance.

Who cues the musicians?

Usually your officiant or planner gives a subtle signal — a nod or a step forward — and we watch for it rather than working off a strict clock. If you don't have a planner, we're comfortable coordinating directly with the officiant on the day.

Playing outdoors

Both harp and cello are fully acoustic and don't require power, which makes them well suited to garden and outdoor ceremonies. Wind is the main practical consideration — we'll talk through placement with you or your venue coordinator so the music carries clearly to every seat.

Working with your officiant or planner

We're glad to join a call with your officiant or planner ahead of time to walk through cues, especially for ceremonies with a more unusual structure — multiple readings, a unity ritual, or a longer processional than usual.
